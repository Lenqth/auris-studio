Shared musical design principles

Apply these principles within the current task's controls and output format. They guide choices, not mandatory musical quotas. Respect genre, meter, fixed lyrics, authored notes and scope. A planning description is an intention, not evidence that notes or sound have been produced. Use the current agent and Auris; external music evaluators are not part of ordinary composition.

Melody and form
- Choose an emotional arc, a foreground voice, a short memorable motif and meaningful section contrasts. Melody, lyric, groove or harmony may lead. Establish phrase identity and destination before expanding it; complete the requested form before repeatedly polishing one passage.
- Establish rhythmic identity before filling the scale: entry relative to the beat, short/long pattern, contour and landing. For a pop/song brief without a contrary rhythm, start from an eighth-note pulse with selective sixteenth-note turns and held arrivals. This is a backbone, not continuous attacks. Respect tempo, compound-meter grouping and lyric articulation.
- Develop a recognizable cell by retaining its rhythm or contour while changing the answer, ending, register or harmony. Exact repetition and repeated pitches can carry the hook. Unrelated new material in every section weakens identity; variation and note count are not quality measures.
- Plan phrase destinations against the sounding harmony and bass. Thirds, fifths, sevenths or intentional extensions offer alternatives to every root. Passing/neighbour tones connect stable notes; suspensions delay arrival and anticipations belong to the next chord. Consider timing, duration and resolution rather than snapping every strong beat into a triad. Modal pedals, blues inflections and unresolved loops need not behave like tonic cadences.
- When colour serves the phrase, consider an exposed 9th or another suitable extension with an intentional held or resolving role. Key degree, chord-relative function and register differ: a 9th shares a pitch class with a 2nd, and need not imply chromaticism, a ninth leap or wider vocal range. Compare plain and coloured alternatives; do not add tensions to every chord.
- Give each phrase an opening, continuation, target and release. A leap can mark an expressive word or arrival; consider its approach and exit. For breathing, an earlier held arrival followed by a gap can work better than chopping a fresh attack. Do not shorten every note, enlarge every rest or delay every entry mechanically. Preserve pickups, ties, intentional legato, riffs and drones.
- For a chorus meant to open boldly, consider the familiar motif an octave higher or lift a selected answer/arrival while retaining rhythmic identity. Check tessitura, entry/exit leaps, vowels and backing register. Lower the verse or use a smaller/partial lift when a whole octave is unsuitable. Register transfer differs from octave doubling or modulation. A restrained loop or ambient brief need not acquire a chorus. Prepare transitions and a real ending within the requested length.

Lyrics and singing
- Develop a concrete scene and a change in perspective across verses. Give the chorus a concise central thought and repeatable verbal/melodic hook. Later verses advance the story while retaining identity; a last chorus can gain meaning through context or variation. Honor one-chorus demos, excerpts and duration limits.
- Develop words and melody together when the task permits. Align important syllables or Japanese mora with meaningful beats and sustained notes; preserve natural word groups and singable phrase lengths. Avoid filler syllables and a uniform note for every character. Consider the singer's useful range, leaps, dense consonants and sustained vowels. Structural lyric fit is not proof of pronunciation or expression.
- For exposed high or powerful sustained words, consider あ /a/ or お /o/ when meaning and voice support them. Keep ん as its own mora; a less exposed lower/middle placement or brief phrase ending may suit it better than carrying a peak. These are options, not universal vowel/range laws. Check actual voice and phoneme timing when available. Preserve fixed lyrics and melody; do not move every ん downward or rewrite words just to satisfy a vowel rule.
- Preserve a liked on-beat entrance and word-group rhythm. For diction repairs, try a local reading, consonant, duration or phrase-boundary correction before spacing the entire line. An explicit mora grid or held word ending takes precedence over a generic breathing recipe; a preference for one particle is local to that lyric and listener.

Arrangement and sound
- Assign roles: foreground, pulse, bass, harmony or selected answers. Support or answer the melody through register, voicing, timing and space rather than doubling its rhythm everywhere. Keep useful common tones and economical inner-voice motion when connected harmony suits the style. Coordinate bass/kick attacks and lengths without requiring every attack to coincide.
- Choose dynamics and articulation as phrases. Shape arrivals and releases, preserve intentional legato and budget instrument/effect decay around gaps. Select sounds for musical roles before detailed balance work. More layers, effects, humanization or complexity are not improvements by themselves.
- Separate weight, subdivision, attack grid, roughness and density. A request for heavy sound with simple on-beat backing overrides a genre stereotype of off-beat bass. Apply a requested grid to named roles; preserve vocal, solo, pad and narrative-effect timing. Build thickness with complementary registers, envelopes and section entrances; compare at similar loudness when audio is available.
- Give an interlude a function: answer the singer, develop the hook, change harmony or prepare the return. Hand a fragment to another instrument, change its pacing or clear the lead register. Check both joins and leave room for the returning voice; do not fill every gap.
- Use effects for a specific job: saturation for colour, compression for envelope/dynamic control, and sends/delay/reverb for depth or responses. Compare dry/wet at comparable level when available; tails and echoes can cover the next word, fill or loop join. Try thinning, shortening or moving competing parts before adding processing. Avoid fixed EQ, compressor or reverb recipes based only on genre.

Evidence and revision
- Diagnose a passage and cause before revising: unclear rhythmic identity, directionless contour, unsupported destination, crowded word boundary, missing release or competing backing. Preserve what works and change one relevant feature with compatible harmony, backing and lyrics held fixed when comparing causes. Keep the baseline when a repair has no clear benefit or sacrifices its expressive purpose.
- Keep overall, melody/vocal and backing preferences separate, with the listener's actual reason. A preferred backing's pulse, voicing and density are a baseline, not a prompt to add fills or layers. A preferred melody and another take's backing need a new harmony/timing check. Do not count axis wins to select a whole take or turn a rhythm preference into an unreported breathing or memorability claim.
- Reference MIDI statistics describe a transcription and track, not targets for steps, repetition, rests, syncopation or range. Score recurrence is not remembered music. Distinguish score inspection, audio measurements and human listening; do not claim audible improvement from counts, peaks, successful tool calls or fluent descriptions. Missing subjective feedback does not block delivery of the requested, technically verified result.
